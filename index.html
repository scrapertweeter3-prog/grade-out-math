Grade-out rate prices a return pallet

Return-pallet math for resale flips

Return pallets are advertised by their manifest: a list of items, a retail total, a starting bid far below it. The manifest total is the ceiling. The number that sets the profit is the grade-out, the share of the manifest a buyer can resell at all. Two buyers can pay the same $400 for the same pallet and land on opposite sides of break-even, because their grade-outs differ.

Take a standard pallet as the model: $400 buy price, $1,100 of manifest retail. Items that grade out resell at about 70 percent of retail, since open-box pricing is the reason anyone buys a return instead of new. The unsellable remainder still moves in bulk as scrap and parts at about 15 percent of its retail share. Marketplace fees take 15 percent of what sells through the platform. Sorting, photos, listing, packing, and buyer messages run about 12 hours per pallet. That model is enough to price three different buyers at the same auction.

The middle row is the median buyer: ordinary grading, fifteen dollars down, twelve hours spent. The spread between a 30 percent and a 60 percent grade-out is $147 on one pallet, which covers most of the pallet's own price. Listing volume does not close that gap. Grading does, and grading happens in the first four hours with a box cutter.

The pricing rule that falls out of the table: bid against your own grade-out history instead of the manifest. A buyer who grades 45 percent is holding a pallet worth $325, because that buy price is where the same 12 hours clear $5 an hour. At a 30 percent grade-out the same hours need the pallet near $250. Manifest retail is the seller's number. Grade-out is the buyer's, and it is the one that sets the rate. That is the same take-home-per-hour accounting behind the gig rankings on Extra Hustles; pallet flipping is the version that comes on a forklift.
